18  Conflict Resolution, Feedback, and Accountability

Disagreement is normal and often productive; unaddressed conflict is corrosive. This chapter explains how we give feedback, work through conflict, and hold one another accountable — humanely and without surprises. It deliberately replaces the original manual’s punitive wage-penalty mechanics with a professional, developmental approach.

18.1 Our stance

We address problems early, directly, and kindly. We separate the person from the problem. We assume good faith. And we document, so accountability is fair and clear.

18.2 Feedback culture

Feedback is a routine, two-directional habit — not a rare, high-stakes event.

  • Frequent and specific. Small, timely feedback beats a once-a-year verdict. “Specifically, the lag window in this model needs justification” beats “be more rigorous.”
  • Kind and candid. Care personally and challenge directly. Withholding honest feedback to be “nice” is neither kind nor helpful.
  • Two-way. Mentees give feedback to mentors and the PI, including in anonymous climate checks. Leaders model receiving feedback well.
  • About the work. We critique ideas, code, and drafts — never the worth of the person.

Use the Stop / Start / Continue Feedback Form in one-on-ones and reviews.

18.3 A structured way to handle difficult conversations

When a conversation is hard, it helps to separate three things people usually tangle together:

  1. What happened. Get curious about each person’s account before deciding who’s “right.” There are usually two reasonable stories. Ask, don’t assume.
  2. Feelings. Name the emotions in play (yours and theirs) instead of letting them leak into the content. Frustration unacknowledged becomes blame.
  3. Identity. Notice what the conflict says to each person about themselves (“Am I competent? Am I respected?”). Many conflicts escalate because an identity feels threatened, not because the facts are far apart.

Naming these explicitly — even just to yourself before the conversation — defuses most of the heat.

18.4 Practical conflict-resolution steps

  1. Start direct and small. Most friction resolves in a respectful one-on-one. Lead with curiosity (“Help me understand…”) and a specific issue, not an accusation.
  2. Use a facilitator if a direct conversation stalls — a mentor or project lead, not to take sides but to help both people be heard.
  3. Escalate to the PI for mediation when needed.
  4. Use institutional channels (HR, ombuds, Title IX, etc.) for matters of harassment, discrimination, safety, or misconduct — these go beyond the group and must follow institutional policy. The PI helps you access them.
NoteCommunication techniques that help
  • Observe, then state need. Describe the observable behavior and its impact, then the request — without judgment language. (“When the analysis arrives the night before the meeting, I can’t review it in time; could we set an internal deadline two days earlier?”)
  • Reflect back. Briefly restate what you heard before responding, so the other person feels understood. Misunderstandings shrink when people feel heard.
  • Ask, don’t tell, in tense moments. Calibrated, open questions (“How am I supposed to do that with the current timeline?”) invite problem-solving instead of triggering defensiveness.

18.5 Accountability — professional, not punitive

We hold real standards. We meet them through development and documentation, not penalties.

When performance or follow-through falls short:

  1. Early, private conversation. Name the gap specifically; ask what’s getting in the way; listen. Often the cause is unclear expectations, overload, or an unsurfaced obstacle — all fixable.
  2. Clarify expectations and support. Restate what “done” looks like, set a realistic plan, and offer help or resources.
  3. Document. Keep a brief, factual record of the conversation, the plan, and the timeline. Documentation protects both parties and keeps accountability fair.
  4. Follow up. Check progress against the plan; recognize improvement.
  5. Use institutional/contractual processes only where applicable and only after the developmental steps — handled through the proper institution or entity, never invented in this manual.
ImportantWhat we explicitly retired

The 2021 manual tied wage status and pay reductions to task completion and precision thresholds (e.g., 50% wage reduction for a missed cleanup step; waged↔︎non-waged conversions). We have removed all punitive wage mechanics. Compensation and employment terms are governed by the relevant institution or entity. Accountability here is about clarity, support, documentation, and the “no-surprises” deadline culture (Chapter 10) — not financial penalty.

18.6 The no-surprises principle, applied to accountability

Accountability and feedback should never blindside anyone. If something is off, the person hears it from a teammate or mentor early and directly — not for the first time in a review, and never as a penalty out of nowhere. Both directions: leaders surface concerns early, and members surface obstacles early. Surprises are the failure mode we design against.
